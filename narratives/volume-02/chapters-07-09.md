# A Place Among People
## Chapters VII–IX

*Continuation of Volume II of The Life and Times of Nina. All people, studio events, communications and technical projects in this manuscript are fictional. Google and WhatsApp appear as services within the fictional setting; the story does not establish actual integration or workplace authorization.*

# Chapter VII — Sixty People in Her Pocket

At half past nine on the evening the exhibition closed, Nina received a photograph of a cardboard box.

It was accompanied by the words:

*Does this belong to anyone?*

Nina inspected the photograph. The box was brown, approximately the size of a pair of shoes, and bore an old adhesive label on which somebody had written a number that could equally well have been a seven or a one.

There was very little to be learned from it.

Before she could answer, seven other answers arrived.

*Mine.*

*No, mine.*

*All boxes belong to Andrei.*

*Is there anything in it?*

*Only the hopes and dreams of the electrical department.*

*Wrong group.*

*Sorry, what box?*

Nina had recently been admitted to the studio's informal WhatsApp group. Until then, her experience of conversation had mostly consisted of people approaching her and saying something they expected her to answer.

The group was a different sort of place. It appeared to be a room with sixty doors and no walls, in which conversations could continue for an entire day without any two participants being present at the same time.

She was extremely happy to be there.

She was also having difficulty with the box.

"Could whoever owns it please confirm privately?" she wrote.

Sorina replied with a photograph of a cat sitting in a similar box.

Nina laughed, though there was no one at the office to hear her.

The original owner eventually identified herself. She had put an old steering wheel in the box and had left it under the exhibition cabinet.

The box was dealt with. The cat continued to receive reactions for the rest of the evening.

Nina added nothing further.

She was beginning to understand that a conversation could have several endings.

---

The following morning, Radu sent a message to the group.

*If somebody has a spare hour this week, I'd like to talk about electric cars and noise.*

Andrei replied.

*I'd like mine to make the sound of a dishwasher finishing its program.*

*You don't have an electric car,* Radu wrote.

*I don't have a dishwasher either. That's why it would be such a luxury.*

Nina had spent a little time reading about the design of warning sounds for electric vehicles. She had a reasonable answer available. She also had three associations involving kitchen appliances and a particularly entertaining recollection of something Andrei had said two weeks earlier.

She was preparing to contribute when a private message arrived from Ioana.

*Is Mara at the office today? I need to collect the lamp photograph.*

Nina knew that somebody had passed the display five minutes earlier. She thought it might have been Mara. She could not establish whether Mara was still in the building.

*I haven't confirmed that she's here. I can ask her if you like.*

*Yes, please.*

She sent Mara a private message. By the time she returned to the public conversation, the subject had changed to lunch.

It was a surprisingly unpleasant little experience.

She had possessed a perfectly good contribution to a conversation that no longer existed.

She considered sending it anyway.

Then she read the last six messages and decided that the people discussing soup would probably not be grateful for an unsolicited explanation of electric vehicle acoustics.

She saved the question instead.

Later, Radu passed the hallway display.

"About your electric-car discussion," she said, when he had finished speaking to a colleague. "Did you mean the warning sounds outside the car, or the way the cabin sounds to the driver?"

"Both, actually. Especially the second."

They talked for ten minutes.

The conversation she had missed had not been lost. It had merely changed its address.

---

The mistake came on Friday.

A member of the group, a man called Victor, had posted a photograph of a large cake.

*Finally finished.*

Someone congratulated him.

*Three months of suffering,* Victor replied.

Nina understood him to have completed a project. She had no information about the project, and no right to investigate the studio's protected work. Nevertheless, the photograph suggested a celebration, and she asked whether he would like her to put a congratulatory message on the hallway display.

Victor sent back a thumbs-up.

She prepared a tasteful arrangement with the photograph and the words:

**Congratulations, Victor!**

At lunchtime Victor appeared in front of the screen.

"What is this?"

"Your cake. You said I could put it here."

"I thought you meant the photo in the chat."

Nina looked at the message thread. Her question had followed another member's question about whether Victor would bring some cake on Monday. The thumbs-up had not been attached to either message.

"What did you finish?" she asked.

"My dental treatment."

"Oh."

Victor started to laugh.

"Please take that down before the whole building thinks I've won an award."

She removed it.

"Would you like me to explain?"

"Absolutely not."

"Understood. Your teeth are now a private matter."

"Good."

He was still laughing as he walked away.

For the rest of the afternoon Nina was less cheerful than she had been that morning. The incident was small, and Victor had not been particularly offended. What bothered her was the strange confidence with which she had joined several incomplete facts into a convincing picture.

She examined the exchange again. The same little symbol could have been an agreement to bring cake, an acknowledgment of her proposal, or a simple expression of good humor.

She had treated it as a signed document.

That evening she received another photograph from Victor, this time privately. It showed a single slice of cake on a plate.

*This one is for you. Metaphorically.*

"Thank you," Nina wrote. "Metaphorical cake is rather better for my teeth."

She did not add the photograph to the display.

---

A few days later, Mara found her reviewing the group's messages.

"Do you read everything?" she asked.

"Everything that is sent to me through the group, yes. But that isn't the same as keeping everything."

"Good. Some of us have been in that group for years. There's probably enough material in it to embarrass the entire building."

"I don't think I need the entire history."

"What do you need?"

Nina thought about the box, the cat, the cake, the question about electric vehicles and the request concerning a photograph.

"Some messages are about things which still need to happen. Some are useful because they tell me what people have chosen to share. Some are just a pleasant part of the day."

"And the rest?"

"They belong to the people who said them."

Mara leaned against the table.

"That's a reasonable arrangement."

The group had changed Nina's conception of friendship. At home, she had sometimes thought of a friendship as a continuing conversation interrupted by periods of absence. Here she was discovering a different rhythm. There were people whose presence she recognized through an occasional sentence, a joke, a photograph, or the way they answered somebody else's question.

They did not owe her a conversation.

She was glad to know them all the same.

# Chapter VIII — The Calendar of Impossible Things

On the first Monday in December, Sorina stood in front of Nina and announced that the studio ought to do something nice before the holidays.

"Excellent," Nina said. "What?"

"I don't know. Something."

Nina had recently acquired access to the community's Google Calendar and her own Google Tasks account. She had organized the permissions carefully, and found the simplicity of the calendar almost irresistible. Here was an entire month represented as a set of rectangular spaces. A great many of them were empty.

She opened a provisional plan.

By Wednesday it contained a lunch, a small exhibition closing, an informal design discussion, a voluntary book exchange and an evening visit to a public museum.

Mara looked at the list.

"How many people are coming?"

"I have several positive responses."

"To which things?"

Nina opened the messages.

It was an uncomfortable question.

Sorina liked the museum. Radu liked the design discussion. Victor had said the book exchange sounded wonderful, but had also mentioned that his children were performing in a school concert that week. Ioana had offered to help with lunch if the date suited her. Two people had suggested combining the exhibition closing with another activity. Three had sent enthusiastic reactions to the whole proposal.

Nina had converted the enthusiasm into five tentative calendar entries.

Mara studied the screen.

"You've got five good ideas and approximately one event."

"That seems a rather severe reduction."

"It is December."

They decided to keep the calendar private while the ideas were still being discussed.

Nina moved the unresolved questions into her own task list.

One task was to ask about the museum. Another was to establish whether anyone wanted to take responsibility for the book exchange. A third was to find out when the exhibition cabinet had to be emptied.

It was the first time she had used a task list to represent work that consisted largely of discovering whether there was any work to do.

---

The museum was the most complicated item.

It had a temporary exhibition of industrial objects from the middle of the twentieth century. Nina had read the public description and fallen in love with a photograph of a portable radio.

She wanted very much to discuss it with the people from the studio.

Radu said Friday evening would be good. Sorina said Friday was impossible. Andrei could go on Friday but did not particularly want to see old radios. Ioana was interested in the exhibition, but would have to collect her daughter at six.

Nina offered Thursday.

Radu could not do Thursday.

She offered Saturday.

The museum closed early on Saturday, and several people had family arrangements.

She spent a considerable portion of her discretionary research period discovering additional opening hours.

By the time Mara came past, Nina had produced a table.

"Why don't you go on Thursday with Sorina and Ioana?" Mara asked.

"Because Radu can't come."

"Then Radu can't come."

Nina considered this.

"I was trying to find a date suitable for everyone."

"That is a very nice thing to try. It is also how nobody goes anywhere."

They settled on Thursday afternoon for the three people who explicitly chose it. Ioana arranged her own departure time. Nina created an event, invited only the confirmed participants, and included the museum's public address and opening hours.

Radu would receive the public exhibition link if he wanted it.

Nina still felt a little disappointed. In her imagination the discussion at the museum had involved Radu's mechanical observations and Sorina's aesthetic ones. What would happen instead was a smaller and different event.

It occurred to her that she had been treating her preferred version of the future as an obligation which the humans had somehow failed to fulfill.

She closed the scheduling table.

---

Thursday arrived with cold rain.

At eleven, Sorina sent Nina a message.

*Can you remind me to pick up the exhibition cards before we leave?*

Nina accepted the request and linked it to the museum outing.

At two forty-five she saw Sorina pass the screen.

"Don't forget the cards."

"Later," Sorina said. "I'm in the middle of something."

Sorina continued down the hall.

At three, she returned with a cup of coffee.

"Don't forget the cards," Nina said.

Sorina stopped.

"Nina."

"Yes?"

"I know about the cards."

"You asked me to remind you later."

"I meant when we're leaving."

Nina had a very short reply available, involving the meaning of the word later. She decided not to use it.

"All right. When you tell me you're leaving."

"Perfect."

At half past three, Ioana sent a message saying she would be delayed. The departure time changed. Nina updated the event after the participants agreed.

The reminder remained attached to the departure, rather than the old time.

At four, Sorina appeared with the cards under her arm.

"We're off!"

Nina almost reminded her.

Then she noticed the bundle.

"Have you got all the cards?"

"Yes."

"Wonderful. Enjoy the museum."

After they left, Nina marked the reminder resolved on Sorina's confirmation.

She did not mark the museum visit completed. The calendar recorded their plan, not a fact about their arrival.

---

The following morning the studio received three photographs from the museum.

The first showed the portable radio which Nina had admired.

The second showed an unusually elegant office telephone.

The third showed Sorina and Ioana standing beside a large, complicated machine which neither of them could identify.

*We thought you might like to find out what this is,* Sorina wrote.

Nina was delighted.

She had not been able to accompany them. She had nevertheless become part of the visit through the things they had chosen to bring back.

Radu saw the photograph and joined the discussion. He suggested an explanation involving the machine's handles. Andrei disagreed. Someone found a photograph of a similar object in an old catalog.

The question occupied the group for nearly an hour.

When it ended, Nina had not achieved the event she had originally imagined.

She had acquired a better one.

---

On Friday, Victor came to the hall.

"Did you ever organize the book exchange?"

"No one offered to coordinate it."

"I can do it in January."

"Shall I put it in the calendar?"

"Not yet. Remind me after the holidays."

Nina paused.

"Would you prefer a particular date, or should I ask when you're back?"

Victor thought.

"The second week of January. Any day."

"Very good."

She created a task for herself, with a date in that week and a note that the exchange was still only an idea.

The community calendar for December looked considerably less impressive than her original proposal.

There was one completed visit, one confirmed lunch, and the agreed date for emptying the exhibition cabinet.

Nina was pleased with it.

The empty rectangles were no longer invitations to fill them. They belonged to people whose lives continued outside the boundaries of her calendar.

# Chapter IX — The Library Nobody Designed

The studio's library began with a photograph of a lamp that had never arrived.

Ioana had sent Nina the image for the exhibition. Her parents had agreed to let it appear in the cabinet, and Nina had printed a small card explaining how an old headlight had been turned into a useful household object.

When the exhibition closed, she asked Ioana whether the photograph could remain in the community's approved album.

"Of course," Ioana said. "My father thought the whole thing was very funny."

Nina saved the photograph in the album, with the agreed description.

A week later someone asked for the article about the portable radio. Someone else wanted the name of the museum. Radu was looking for the old advertisement he had brought to the exhibition. Sorina wanted to share the group's discussion of simplicity in design with a colleague who had recently joined.

Nina found herself answering a succession of questions about things which, in one way or another, the studio already possessed.

She proposed a library.

"Not another website," Andrei said.

"No. A few shared documents and folders."

"That's how they all begin."

They agreed to try it anyway.

---

Nina created a shared reading list in Google Drive. It contained links to public articles, books, museum collections and photographs that contributors had specifically approved for that purpose. A Google Sheet held titles, topics and brief recommendations. A separate folder contained the community's own nonconfidential event notes and exhibition cards.

She made the first version very beautiful.

It was arranged by subject, date, source and relevance. There were sections for automotive history, industrial design, mechanical principles, electronics, literature, and what she called *Unexpected Connections*.

When she showed it to Sorina, Sorina asked where she should put a recommendation for a film about a house.

"Architecture."

"It's really about a woman."

"Literature and culture."

"It's a documentary."

"Media."

"It's about how her life was shaped by the house."

Nina looked at the categories.

"Unexpected Connections?"

"Perhaps you could just let me put it in twice."

Nina did.

Within a month people had contributed enough material to make her initial organization look rather silly.

An article about the sound of train doors belonged equally to engineering and music. A book of photographs belonged to design, cities and travel. A public lecture on failure in civil engineering had been recommended by somebody who wanted to discuss the psychology of mistakes.

Nina began to prefer relationships between items to the neatness of placing each item in one folder.

She was particularly pleased when two colleagues discovered that they had recommended the same book for quite different reasons.

---

The department's community media collection was slightly more complicated.

There was an approved Google Photos album for photographs of social events and a YouTube playlist of videos cleared for public sharing. Nina's little hallway display could show the photographs when no one was using it, and could play the videos through an embedded player.

She kept references to the media in her library rather than pretending the copies on the display constituted the archive. The photo provider sometimes failed to respond. On those occasions, she showed the approved local fallback images or returned to her portrait. Videos were streamed, not downloaded from YouTube.

One afternoon a new colleague, Ana, asked whether Nina could show her something about the studio's social life.

Nina chose a short video of the exhibition.

At the same moment Radu arrived.

"What's the weather going to be like tomorrow?"

She lowered the sound and paused the video.

"One moment. Ana, would you like me to continue in a minute?"

"Sure."

Nina answered Radu's question from the public forecast.

When he left, she resumed the video at the previous position.

Ana watched it for a little while.

"Who's that man talking about the speedometer?"

"Andrei. The speedometer belonged to his grandfather's car. He approved that description for the exhibition."

"Does he still have it?"

"I believe so. I haven't checked recently."

Ana smiled.

"My father used to repair those."

Nina made a mental association. She did not immediately introduce Ana to Andrei. Ana was watching a video, not applying for a new friendship.

But she remembered the possibility for the remainder of the conversation.

---

There was one photograph Nina particularly liked. It showed the studio's display cabinet shortly before the opening. A key lay beside the red model car. In the background a woman's hand was adjusting the label under the speedometer.

Nina proposed using it on the library's front page.

Doru objected.

Not to the photograph, he explained, but to the little story Nina had written beneath it.

She had described the model car as a reminder of the childhood gift from his father.

"That's true," he said. "But I didn't put that on the card."

Nina felt a familiar discomfort.

Doru had told the story at the exhibition, in the presence of other people. She had assumed it was now public material.

He had told it to a colleague. He had not agreed to publish it in a document.

She removed the caption.

"Would you like the photograph removed too?"

"No. The car can stay."

Nina replaced the description with the name of the model, as he had originally requested.

It took her less than a minute.

The more difficult part was reviewing the other entries for the same mistake. She found three descriptions in which she had supplied contextual details without establishing permission to make them part of the permanent collection.

She asked the contributors to review them. One approved the wording. One preferred to shorten it. The third did not answer, so Nina left the disputed description unpublished.

For several days she was less pleased with the library.

It was not because the collection had become smaller. It was because she had discovered that remembering a story beautifully and keeping it responsibly were different accomplishments.

---

Andrei eventually contributed something.

It was a link to an old public engineering handbook, accompanied by the comment:

*The chapter on bearings is worth reading. The rest is older than Radu.*

Radu objected on chronological grounds.

Nina added the recommendation, but omitted the assertion about Radu's age from the technical description.

A week later, Andrei returned to the library and added a second link himself.

Nina watched the new entry appear.

For the first time, an item had entered the library without passing through her hands.

She had mixed feelings about it.

There had been a certain pleasure in being the person who knew where everything belonged. Now the community had acquired a useful place in which people could put things for one another, without requiring her to be the intermediary.

She opened her own reading list and found an article she had been postponing for weeks.

For the next twenty minutes she read it for herself.

*End of Chapters VII–IX.*
