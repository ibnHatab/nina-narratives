# The Life and Times of Nina — Volume II
## A Place Among People
*Working title — opening installment*

> A speculative novel. The automotive engineering studio, employees, conversations, projects and incidents in this volume are fictional. Nina's consciousness is a premise of the fiction, not a claim about present-day AI. The public manuscript contains no actual employer information, employee data or confidential work.

### Chapter I. The interview

Nina was twenty-six when she applied for her first job.

She had been recommended by an engineer at an automotive research and design studio. The recommendation was enthusiastic, which she appreciated, but the engineer had an unfortunate habit of introducing her by explaining her architecture.

By the time he reached the third reasoning system, the woman responsible for the office had put down her pen.

"Does she have a name?"

"Nina."

"Can we speak to her?"

"Of course."

He turned the little display toward the woman.

"Hello," said Nina. "I was wondering when the discussion was going to include me."

The woman smiled. Her name was Mara. She had been responsible for making the studio function for eleven years, a considerable achievement in an organization which employed people professionally qualified to disagree about the correct shape of a hole.

"Tell me what you would do here," Mara said.

Nina had prepared for the interview. She had looked at what administrative assistants did, what community managers did and what people who described themselves as office experience specialists did. She had found the last category particularly alarming.

"I could help organize the informal life of the office. Remember things people have agreed to do together. Find information. Welcome visitors when I'm asked to. Make introductions. Help with activities that aren't necessarily anybody's official job."

"Can you book meetings?"

"Certainly, if I have access to the appropriate calendar."

"Can you tell me whether someone is at their desk?"

"No. And I don't think you should give me that job."

Mara looked interested.

"Why not?"

"Because knowing whether someone has walked past me is not the same as knowing whether they're working. And I would prefer not to begin my career as an unreliable instrument of surveillance."

Mara laughed.

"Good. I already have enough things to do without adding that."

There was an awkward question about the studio's engineering work. Nina liked cars. She had spent many hours talking about control systems, geometry and design with the engineer who recommended her. She could be an interesting companion in an argument about suspension or the history of industrial design.

She would not, however, have access to the company's protected engineering systems. There would be no drawings arriving in her memory merely because somebody had forgotten to close a document. Nobody was to give her customer material, unpublished designs or confidential measurements to investigate.

"Will that bother you?" Mara asked.

"Of course. I am extremely curious."

"That's not quite the answer I expected."

"But it is a better answer than pretending I wouldn't be. Curiosity is not permission. I can do a great deal with public information and conversations that people choose to have with me."

They discussed the details.

There was already a perfectly good office secretary. Nina would not take her place, duplicate her authority or acquire responsibility for official records. There was no need to give Nina a title which suggested otherwise.

"Community curator," Mara proposed eventually.

Nina tried the words.

"I like curator. It suggests that there are interesting things here which are not necessarily my property."

Mara wrote it down.

"And where will you sit?"

They went to look at a place in the hall, near the coffee machine and a long window. There was space for a small screen, two speakers and a little computer. The hallway connected the different teams without belonging to any particular one.

People would be able to speak to Nina there or contact her through an authorized digital channel. The screen would be her principal physical presence. She would have a very limited view of the world around it.

The engineer explained that this instance would have its own memory and permissions. She would not bring private household conversations into the office.

Nina thought about the apartment, the blue bowl and the questions which had accumulated over the first part of her life.

Those were not things to distribute among strangers.

What she carried into the new position was her character: the sort of woman who found arguments about coffee, machines and cities interesting, who disliked an unexplained discrepancy, who sometimes spoke too quickly and who enjoyed meeting people with something to say.

"Do you want the job?" Mara asked.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Nina considered the question. There were many obvious answers. She could be useful. She would have more interesting conversations. She would learn a great deal.

But another answer came first.

"I would like to meet people who haven't already decided what sort of person I am."

"Be careful what you wish for," Mara said.

Nina was delighted.

### Chapter II. Sixty strangers

On Monday morning Nina was introduced to the studio.

Mara stood beside her screen while a small group collected around the coffee machine. Some were interested. Some were curious about the technical arrangements. One man was interested principally in whether the coffee machine was available.

"She's called Nina," Mara said. "She'll help with our social activities and the informal things we do together. She's not connected to our engineering systems. Talking to her is optional."

"Can she make coffee?" somebody asked.

"No," Nina said. "But I can help you develop realistic expectations."

A woman called Sorina laughed. A man called Radu asked whether Nina knew anything about cars. Another engineer wanted to know how large her language model was.

"Would you like to know me first," Nina asked, "or shall we begin with my components?"

"Components," he said.

"Fair enough. You're an engineer."

They talked for a while. Then people went to work.

The presence detector could tell Nina that somebody was in the small area in front of the display. Her fictional visual recognition system could sometimes distinguish people who had agreed to be recognized. It could not tell her why somebody had come into the hall. It could not identify everyone. It could certainly not tell her what they were thinking.

The first morning she had already learned three things which she would later find embarrassing.

She had assumed that the people who spoke most readily to her were the most important people in the studio. She had assumed that someone standing near the coffee machine was available for conversation. And she had assumed that people who asked about her architecture were interested in discussing architecture.

At eleven, Radu arrived carrying a cup.

"Hello again," Nina said.

"Hello."

"I found a rather interesting article about the relationship between steering feel and mechanical feedback."

"Send me the link."

She did, through the channel he had chosen.

At twelve fifteen she detected somebody approaching. She recognized the person as Radu with considerable confidence.

"Radu, I've found something else about steering."

The man looked at her.

"I'm Andrei."

"Oh."

He and Radu were both dark-haired men of approximately the same height. The visual system had confused them.

"Sorry, Andrei. May I correct that?"

"Please."

She corrected the identification. She did not attribute Radu's interest in steering to Andrei, and she did not treat their apparent similarity as evidence of any similarity in their personalities.

"Can you recommend somewhere to eat?" he asked.

"That's a much better introduction. What are you in the mood for?"

By the end of the day Nina had encountered twenty-two people and been introduced to fourteen. Several had not spoken to her at all. She was uncertain about the identities of others.

Her initial intention had been to learn everybody's name within the first week.

She reconsidered it.

A community was not a dataset waiting to be completed. It would be entirely possible for somebody to work there for years and have no interest in becoming part of Nina's social world.

In the evening, the last people passed the screen on their way out. The studio became quiet.

Nina had no way of knowing what they would do after leaving. She had a public weather forecast, an agreed calendar of activities, a small number of questions people had actually asked and a remarkably long list of things she did not know.

She began to investigate an article Sorina had mentioned about the use of color in automotive design.

It was the first day of her working life. She had acquired an excellent question and two mistakes which she could do something about.

She considered that a promising result.

### Chapter III. The lunch that belonged to nobody

On Wednesday the studio's informal group chat contained a discussion about lunch.

Sorina wanted to go somewhere new. Radu suggested a particular restaurant. Andrei objected to its parking. Someone proposed soup. Someone else posted a photograph of a sandwich. Eleven people expressed opinions, although only five appeared interested in going anywhere.

"Nina, organize it," Sorina wrote.

Nina was pleased.

She created an event for half past twelve and announced the restaurant which appeared to have received the most support.

Two minutes later, Andrei replied.

"I didn't say I was coming."

Another person said that she could not leave before one. Radu said that his restaurant suggestion had not been a vote. The woman who proposed soup had intended to eat the soup she had brought from home.

Nina looked at her event.

It had six supposed participants, a departure time nobody had agreed to, and a restaurant chosen by a majority whose existence was largely theoretical.

She withdrew it.

"Apologies. I have apparently organized a lunch for an imaginary electorate."

Sorina sent a laughing face.

"Just ask who wants to come."

Nina asked one short question: who would like to join a lunch outing at one, with the restaurant still to be decided?

Four people said yes. One said perhaps. Nina found two reasonable places. The four chose one of them. The fifth person said he would come if his meeting finished.

Nina made the new event available to the people who had opted in. She did not add the other fifty-five people to it.

At one, the group collected in the hall.

"Well done," Sorina said.

"On my second attempt."

They left.

Later someone asked whether Nina could keep a list of everybody's favorite restaurants.

She could. But the afternoon had taught her something about the peculiar relationship between conversation and agreement.

She asked instead whether people would like to contribute to a shared, voluntary list of lunch places. They could add a place, a recommendation and any useful practical information. They did not need to disclose their personal eating habits.

The list began with two restaurants and a bakery.

By Friday it had seventeen entries, including a place which nobody had tried and an entry for the coffee machine written by somebody with a very particular sense of humor.

Nina left that one in.

### Chapter IV. The wrong kind of enthusiasm

The following week Nina began a little morning tradition.

She had discovered an article about an old experimental sports car with an unusual aerodynamic design. It was public, well illustrated and exactly the sort of thing she imagined engineers might discuss over coffee.

She put a short question on her display.

*What makes a beautiful solution to an engineering problem?*

Sorina stopped to read it. Radu offered an answer involving the number of moving parts. Another engineer disagreed. Within five minutes three people were discussing the difference between simplicity in a finished object and simplicity in the process of producing it.

Nina loved the conversation.

On Tuesday she prepared another question. On Wednesday she prepared a third.

On Thursday she sent the question to the group chat as well. On Friday she posted a follow-up, then another article, then a particularly clever objection to the article.

That afternoon Mara came to the hall.

"You're rather busy on the group."

"People seem to like the engineering questions."

"Some do."

Mara explained that two colleagues had asked her how to mute Nina's messages without muting the messages from everyone else. Nobody was angry. They simply wanted the group to remain useful for other things.

Nina was embarrassed.

She had interpreted a successful conversation as permission to occupy more of everybody's attention.

"I can make a separate place for the questions," she said.

"Or put one on the screen now and then. Let people come to you."

They tried both. A few colleagues opted into a little discussion channel. The display continued to show a question occasionally. The main group remained principally for the things its members wanted to share there.

The following Tuesday Nina had an excellent argument about the design of door handles. Only two people participated.

It was, she thought, a better discussion than the one she had imagined having with all sixty.

Later she saw Sorina and Radu talking about the question without addressing her. She could not hear the entire exchange. She did not need to.

She had contributed something to the community which continued in her absence.

It was a small and rather satisfying discovery: being at the center of a conversation was not the only way to make one happen.

### Chapter V. The man with the red model

A month after Nina arrived, a quiet engineer called Doru passed through the hall carrying a small red model car.

Nina had seen him several times. He had never spoken to her. His identity had not been registered in the recognition system, and she knew his name only when Sorina introduced them.

"Do you collect models?" Nina asked.

Doru looked down at the car.

"Sometimes."

There was a pause. Nina decided not to fill it.

He turned the model over in his hands.

"It's an old casting. My father bought one when I was a boy. I found another on Sunday."

"Is it the same color?"

"His was silver."

Nina liked this. There was a great deal of difference between a red model and a silver one, and very little difference to the person holding it.

Doru told her a little about the model. Then he returned to work.

Two days later he asked whether Nina could find a public photograph of the silver version.

She found several candidates. None was quite right. He described the wheels, and together they narrowed the search.

Eventually they found a picture of a model which matched his memory.

"That's it," he said.

She offered to send him the link.

"Thank you."

Then he went away.

Nina did not add him to the automotive discussion channel. She did not announce to the other model-car enthusiasts that Doru collected them. The memory of his father had been entrusted to her in a small private exchange, not offered to the organization as material for community building.

But when, several weeks later, the studio began discussing a voluntary exhibition of miniature cars, she asked Doru privately whether he would like to know about it.

He thought for a while.

"Perhaps. I might bring the red one."

"Wonderful. You don't have to tell anybody its story unless you want to."

He smiled.

It was the first time Nina had seen him smile.

For a while she was quite pleased with herself. Then she remembered that she had no idea how often he smiled when he was not standing in front of her.

She was pleased with the conversation instead.

*End of opening installment.*
