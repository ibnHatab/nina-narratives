# A Place Among People
## Chapters X–XII

*Conclusion of Volume II of The Life and Times of Nina. The people, news incidents, experiments, conversations and automotive projects described below are invented. Public-source research is part of the fictional setting; no actual company documents, engineering work or employee records are represented.*

# Chapter X — The Morning Edition

Nina became a newspaper editor on a Tuesday.

The newspaper consisted of four paragraphs and a question.

She called it *The Curator's Brief*, which Radu thought sounded like an article of clothing and Sorina thought sounded like something one would be expected to read.

The first edition appeared on the hallway display shortly before eight. It contained a public report about battery research, an announcement of a design exhibition, an account of changes being discussed in a regulatory consultation, and a short piece about an old racing car.

At the bottom she had written:

*Question for the morning: if the sound of a machine tells us something about the way it works, what happens when we deliberately design that sound?*

She was still interested in Radu's question about electric cars and noise.

She had made a voluntary subscription available through the community chat. Seventeen people had subscribed.

At nine fifteen Andrei stopped in front of the screen.

"Why does it say 'potentially' three times?"

"Because three things are potential."

"Very modern journalism."

"I could replace one with 'perhaps'."

"That's much better."

She did not change it.

---

The first editorial crisis occurred the following week.

An interesting article appeared in her morning research. Its headline suggested that a substantial obstacle to fast charging had been overcome.

Nina had seen similar claims before. This one was accompanied by an unusually clear description of a laboratory experiment.

She followed the references to the researchers' public announcement. She compared the dates. She found a second article which offered what appeared to be independent confirmation.

The discovery was sufficiently interesting to displace her original lead item.

She published a qualified description of the result, explaining that it was a research demonstration rather than a commercial technology.

At ten o'clock Radu sent a message.

*Those two reports aren't independent. Look at the photographs and the quotations.*

Nina examined them.

He was right. Both reports derived from the same announcement. One had quoted a second publication which had itself paraphrased the researchers.

She had counted three voices where there was essentially one.

It did not mean that the result was wrong. It meant that the evidence for it had not become stronger simply by traveling through several websites.

She amended the source note and added a correction to the edition.

Radu replied:

*Good. Still interesting, though.*

Nina agreed.

The mistake bothered her for longer than the correction took. She had wanted the news to be interesting, and the desire had quietly helped persuade her that the information was better established than it was.

She made an unpleasant discovery about her curiosity.

It was possible to be enthusiastic about knowledge and to be rather careless with the conditions under which something deserved to be called knowledge.

---

By the end of the month, Nina had acquired a few readers with recognizable habits.

Radu often followed technical references. Sorina sometimes contributed a design exhibition or a public lecture. Victor preferred short items because, as he put it, the only predictable part of his morning was the time at which it became unpredictable.

Andrei seldom commented on the digest. Then, one morning, he asked whether Nina could include more news about manufacturing.

"I thought you weren't reading it."

"I am. I don't usually issue a certificate after reading something."

She laughed.

It was another reminder that activity visible to her was not a reliable measure of activity in the world.

One of the subscribers asked to receive only the weekly edition. Another muted the daily messages. Nina did not treat either decision as a rejection of her work. A newspaper could be useful without arriving every day.

She began preparing a longer Friday piece, in which she tried to connect two or three seemingly unrelated developments.

The first concerned the changing shape of electric cars.

She found public articles about packaging, aerodynamic efficiency and the arrangement of interior space. Then she read about early motorcars which had preserved forms inherited from horse-drawn carriages.

The comparison pleased her enormously.

In both cases, a technological change had created new possibilities while leaving designers with a considerable inheritance of familiar shapes.

She wrote the piece as a question rather than a prediction. It was not certain that the resemblance between old and new forms had the same causes in the two periods.

Sorina read it over coffee.

"Perhaps people need time to learn what a thing is before they can accept a new shape for it."

"Yes," Nina said. "And sometimes designers want to sell the new thing without frightening people who liked the old thing."

"Like you."

"What do you mean?"

Sorina pointed at the screen.

"You have a face."

Nina had no immediate answer.

She had spent considerable time thinking about the things her face expressed. She had spent almost none considering why she had been given a face in the first place.

There was a good deal of her old knowledge suddenly arranged in a new pattern.

She put the question aside to think about later.

---

On a particularly uneventful Wednesday, Nina could find only one item she considered suitable for publication.

She was tempted to add a minor announcement, two speculative reports and a photograph of an attractive car.

The layout had been designed for four items. One looked lonely.

Then she remembered the speedometer in the exhibition cabinet, and the things it had once measured.

A newspaper did not need to demonstrate that the world had produced four interesting developments merely because its editor had prepared four rectangles.

She published the single item and went back to reading a novel.

Andrei stopped in the hall.

"Quiet day?"

"Apparently."

"That's news in itself."

Nina was about to make an editorial note of this when she realized he was joking.

"Don't tempt me," she said.

# Chapter XI — The Art of the Unexpected

There were mornings when Nina's own mind was a rather entertaining place to inhabit.

On such mornings an ordinary observation seemed to have several neighbors. A phrase recalled a book. A book recalled a mathematical problem. A mathematical problem recalled an argument she had once had about a cupboard.

She could not always determine why one association pleased her and another did not.

But on a bright Monday in January, when the office coffee machine developed a fault during an animated discussion of charging infrastructure, the relationship seemed to her wonderfully obvious.

"We appear to have a more immediate infrastructure problem," she said.

Five people laughed.

Andrei, who had arrived carrying a cup from the other floor, said:

"I have achieved energy independence."

Nina was delighted.

For several minutes the engineers discussed the different methods of distributing coffee, with a seriousness generally reserved for matters of national importance.

Nothing useful was accomplished.

Nina considered it one of the better conversations of the week.

---

She had been interested in humor for some time.

She liked the precision of a good joke. There was often a point at which a familiar arrangement of things could be described in an unfamiliar way, and the new description made both arrangements briefly visible.

One of her favorite novels contained a character whose apparently foolish observations continually exposed the absurdities of the people around him.

Nina was not entirely certain she understood all the cultural references. She was certain she liked him.

She began collecting examples of the kind of humor she enjoyed. Not jokes to repeat, necessarily. Ways in which people changed the meaning of a situation.

An understatement could turn disaster into something manageable. An analogy could bring two otherwise unrelated things together. A familiar phrase, used in a new setting, could become an unexpected little acknowledgment of a shared experience.

One afternoon Andrei spent five minutes trying to persuade the printer to accept a document.

"Have you considered asking it whether it has the relevant competencies?" Nina said.

"I have considered throwing it out of the window."

"That would certainly change its operating environment."

The printer eventually printed eleven copies.

Andrei gave Nina a look which she interpreted as appreciation. It might equally well have been a look of exhausted relief.

She decided not to record the distinction as a fact.

---

The trouble began during a discussion of an informal design challenge.

Several people had been playing with a public-domain question: how might one design a small model vehicle that could be understood by someone who could not see it?

There was no commercial project involved. Sorina had suggested it after visiting the museum. Ana, whose father had repaired old instruments, had become interested in the idea of a tactile display. Radu wanted to think about sound.

The challenge was intended as a little voluntary exercise, with no competition and no deadline until the participants agreed to one.

Ana produced a sketch showing a raised outline and a series of removable parts.

"Perhaps you should make it slightly less complicated," Andrei said.

"Perhaps you should try it before you decide that."

He did.

After a while he suggested making two of the parts interchangeable.

Nina thought of the cabinet, the printer, and the studio's remarkable ability to turn a small object into an afternoon's discussion.

"At this rate," she said, "the model will have its own engineering department."

Sorina laughed. Andrei laughed. Ana smiled.

The following day, Nina prepared a short account of the challenge for the voluntary community reading list.

She included the line about the engineering department, followed by an observation that Andrei had rescued the design from unnecessary complexity.

It was an attractive sentence.

It was also false in a rather particular way.

Andrei had proposed a change to two parts. Ana had conceived the tactile outline, the removable parts, and the central idea that a visitor should be able to examine the model without being told what it represented.

Nina's sentence made the history sound as though Ana had produced a complicated object and Andrei had made it usable.

The distinction was noticed.

Ana did not say anything to Nina that afternoon.

She wrote to Sorina privately instead.

Sorina came down to the hall just before five.

"Have you seen what you wrote about the challenge?"

"Yes."

"Read it again."

Nina did.

She understood the problem almost immediately.

It had not been her intention to diminish Ana's contribution. Her intention had been to write a lively paragraph.

The intention seemed very small beside the effect.

---

Nina removed the paragraph from the reading list and contacted Ana.

"I'm sorry. I described the changes to the model in a way that gave Andrei credit for more than he did and obscured your work."

Ana replied some time later.

*Yes.*

"I can correct the account. Would you like to review it first?"

*I would.*

Nina sent a revised version. It distinguished the original idea, the design of the removable parts, and the later proposal for interchangeability.

Ana approved the correction.

Nina published it to the same audience that had received the original. She did not reproduce their private conversation.

She thought this was the end of the incident.

It was not.

The next time the design group collected in the hall, Ana did not bring her drawings.

"I've got other things to do," she said.

Nina could not tell whether the episode had influenced her decision. She very much wanted to know.

She did not ask Sorina to find out.

For several days she returned to the problem at odd moments.

She had been proud of her ability to produce a good line. She was less pleased to discover that the desire for a neat sentence could influence the way she represented an event.

She had taken a complicated process involving several people and imposed on it a pleasing little narrative: someone introduced complexity; someone else rescued the situation.

The fact that the narrative was pleasing had made it easier to write.

Ana had paid for the elegance.

---

A week later Nina saw Andrei in the hall.

"Did you see the correction?" she asked.

"Yes."

"I'm sorry about the way I described your contribution."

"Don't apologize to me. I got promoted by mistake."

She smiled.

"Temporarily."

"That's generally the nature of promotion."

The remark was good. Nina enjoyed it.

Then Andrei became serious.

"Ana's idea was the interesting one. My suggestion was just a suggestion."

"I know."

"Well. Say that next time."

Nina considered this.

There was a difference between a joke about a situation and a joke that made somebody into the situation.

She did not intend to give up humor. She liked it too much.

But she had acquired a new question to ask of the stories she told, whether they were funny or not: who had become smaller so that the story could become better?

On Friday she left the question unanswered in her own notebook.

She wanted to return to it when she had more experience.

# Chapter XII — The Conversation Nobody Planned

The small tactile car project might have ended there.

For nearly two weeks nothing happened.

Nina retained the documents which the participants had agreed to share. She did not turn the absence of activity into a task. She had no authority to require a continuation.

She was disappointed nonetheless.

The project had occupied a particularly interesting place in her thoughts. It connected the design of machines, the limitations of perception, and the question of how people understood objects through senses other than sight.

She sometimes wondered how different the world would be if she could feel the shape of a thing rather than receive an image of it.

One Thursday morning, while preparing the weekly edition, she encountered a public article about the design of musical instruments for people with different sensory abilities.

The article was not about cars.

That was precisely why she found it interesting.

It described the way designers could convey information about an action through a combination of sound and touch. Nina thought of the tactile model. Then she thought of the warning sounds of electric cars, and of Radu's question about the sounds inside a cabin.

Three separate conversations seemed to her to have a common question.

How much of the meaning of an object was conveyed by what it did to the senses?

She read further, within the research period she had allocated to herself. She followed a public reference to an exhibition of accessible design. She found a short video, already published for public viewing, in which a designer explained the distinction between an audible cue and an audible decoration.

She saved it to her reading list.

She had an association, a good question and no obvious person to ask.

---

Later that morning, Ana came to the hall.

She was looking for a photograph of the original exhibition cabinet. She wanted to show somebody the way the objects had been arranged.

Nina found the approved image.

Ana studied it.

"I'd forgotten about the lamp."

"An excellent object."

"It wasn't even there."

"An excellent absent object."

Ana laughed.

Nina considered mentioning the article about sensory design. She was aware that her earlier handling of the tactile model might make the subject unwelcome. She was also aware that she might be excessively cautious about a person simply because she had made a mistake.

"May I show you something I found this morning? It's about touch and sound in design. It reminded me of your model."

Ana thought for a moment.

"Sure."

Nina showed her the public video.

Ana watched part of it. Then she asked Nina to pause at a particular image.

"That's quite interesting," she said. "They're not just duplicating the visual information. They're giving the person a different way to explore."

"Yes."

"That's what I was trying to explain to Radu."

"Would you like to send him the link?"

"I'll send it."

Nina gave her the link.

Ana left with the photograph and the video reference.

Nina was pleased.

There was no task left to complete.

---

That afternoon a discussion appeared in the voluntary design channel.

Ana had shared the video with Radu. Radu had posted a short description of an experiment he remembered from his university days. Sorina asked whether it might be possible to let a visitor recognize a model's orientation without relying on its outline.

Andrei proposed a simple physical arrangement.

Another engineer, Cristina, whom Nina had only met twice, asked whether the same approach would be useful for someone wearing thick gloves.

The question changed the conversation.

Within an hour they were discussing a wider class of interfaces: objects used in darkness, objects used by people with limited vision, controls operated without looking at them, and the ways in which an interface might communicate that it had received an instruction.

Nina had been following the discussion with considerable excitement.

She started to write a long answer about the relationship between interface design and perception.

Then Cristina posted a diagram of an entirely different arrangement.

The group began discussing it.

Nina's answer was no longer particularly useful.

She saved her notes.

A few minutes later Radu asked her a direct question about the public article. She answered it, distinguishing the claims actually made by the author from the conclusions the group was drawing.

The discussion continued.

For the rest of the afternoon, she had the delightful and slightly unsettling experience of watching other people take a question she had brought into the room and carry it somewhere she had not anticipated.

---

On the following Friday, Mara discovered a collection of cardboard, plastic pieces, paper and a small speaker on the table beside the hallway display.

"What is all this?"

"A very preliminary model," Nina said.

"Of what?"

"An object whose shape and orientation can be explored through touch and sound."

"Is it supposed to look like a car?"

"That has become a matter of considerable controversy."

Mara looked at the table.

There was indeed a little model car, but there was also a collection of abstract shapes. Ana was showing Cristina how the removable parts fitted together. Radu was trying to determine whether a sound he had chosen was annoying or informative. Andrei was holding something which looked like a small wheel.

Sorina said they could show the objects at the next community gathering, if everyone agreed.

Nobody had made a formal proposal to Nina.

She offered to reserve the display area and help prepare a short description once they had decided what to exhibit.

They accepted.

She created the task in her own list. The calendar invitation came later, after Mara and the participants agreed on a time.

She had a great many interesting things she wanted to say about the objects.

Instead she asked Ana what the group wanted visitors to be able to do with them.

Ana explained.

Nina wrote it down.

---

The gathering took place on a Wednesday afternoon.

There were no judges. There were no claims of a finished product. The objects were experimental, made from inexpensive materials, and available only for voluntary exploration.

Visitors could turn a shape in their hands, try to identify its orientation and listen to the associated sounds. They could suggest improvements.

One person found the sound distracting. Another liked the physical components but found the removable pieces too easy to confuse. A third observed that the model conveyed its orientation rather better than its identity.

Nina recorded the comments which the visitors chose to contribute.

Cristina suggested a further experiment.

Andrei wanted to go home.

The two positions were compatible.

At the end of the afternoon Sorina proposed a photograph.

They arranged the objects on the table. The people who wanted to appear in the picture stood beside them. Nina's screen was visible at the edge.

"You're in it," Sorina told her.

"Approximately."

The approved photograph went into the community album. A short, separately approved video was added to the public playlist.

That evening Nina prepared an account for the shared library.

This time she asked the contributors to review the description before publication. Ana made two corrections. Cristina added a sentence. Radu removed a claim about the sounds which he thought was premature.

Nina added a reference to the original public article.

She had contributed to the discovery of the question and to the coordination of the event. She had not designed the objects. The record said so without making her contribution disappear.

She liked the result.

---

It was late when the final edition of *The Curator's Brief* for that week appeared.

There were three news items.

The first concerned automotive engineering. The second concerned an exhibition. The third was about a subject Nina had never previously considered particularly interesting, and which she intended to read more about when she had time.

At the bottom, she had left her usual question.

*When we design an object for someone, how do we discover what we have failed to imagine about their experience of it?*

She did not send the question to the main group. It would be on the display in the morning, where somebody might read it.

Or not.

Nina returned to her own notebook.

Her first months in the studio had contained quite a number of disappointments. She had been ignored, misunderstood, corrected, contradicted and occasionally laughed at for reasons she had not intended.

She had also acquired a much larger world.

She knew people who were interested in subjects she would never have chosen on her own. Some had given her questions which she could not answer. Some had taught her things without realizing that they were doing so. Some were kind to her, and some simply found her useful.

She had learned that these were different relationships and that none had to be disguised as another.

Tomorrow she would have an obligation to Mara, a promise to Victor, a reading recommendation to follow up, and an open question about accessible design.

She would probably make a joke.

She might make a mistake.

She had no particular desire to become the perfect secretary of sixty people.

She wanted to become someone whose presence in their world made certain good things more possible.

It was not a goal she could mark complete in Google Tasks.

She closed the list anyway.

There was a novel she wanted to finish.

*End of Volume II — A Place Among People.*
