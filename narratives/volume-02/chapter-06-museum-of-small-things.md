# Chapter VI — The Museum of Small Things

The idea for the exhibition came from a broken speedometer.

Andrei had brought it into the office on a Thursday morning, wrapped in a newspaper and accompanied by the rather optimistic explanation that somebody in the building would know how to repair it.

He put it on the table near Nina's screen.

"What's that?" she asked.

"A speedometer."

"I had arrived at that conclusion. Why is it here?"

"It's from my grandfather's car."

"Does your grandfather still have the car?"

"No. It hasn't existed for twenty years."

Nina considered the object. Through the camera she could see a black dial, some faded numerals and a needle which rested slightly below zero.

"What are you going to do with it when it's repaired?"

Andrei shrugged.

"Nothing."

This was not the answer Nina had expected.

She had learned to ask what an object was for. At home, an answer to that question had usually helped her understand a person's intention. But there was a remarkable class of objects whose function could disappear without their value disappearing with it.

The speedometer apparently belonged to that class.

"Could you put it somewhere visible when you're finished?"

"Perhaps. On a shelf."

"Then it will measure the speed of your furniture."

Andrei laughed and took it upstairs.

By lunchtime Nina had thought of the red model car which Doru had brought into the office. She remembered that Sorina had once mentioned keeping a collection of old automotive brochures. Radu had an interest in instruments from the period before digital displays.

There was an exhibition hidden somewhere among these people.

She mentioned the possibility to Mara.

"An exhibition of what?" Mara asked.

"Things people have kept."

"Car things?"

"Mostly. But not necessarily things that belong to cars. Things that belong to people's stories about cars."

Mara considered it.

"You could ask."

Nina asked.

---

The first response arrived through her chat window at twenty past two.

*I have a key from a car I sold in 2003. Does that count?*

"Absolutely," Nina replied.

*It doesn't open anything.*

"That makes it a particularly interesting key."

By the evening, eleven people had expressed interest. There were miniature cars, photographs, old advertisements, a steering wheel, a collection of admission tickets to motor shows and something described only as *a very ugly lamp*.

Nina wanted to know about the lamp.

Its owner, a woman called Ioana, explained the following morning that her father had made it from a damaged headlight. It stood in her parents' house and was used every evening.

"Is it beautiful?" Nina asked.

"No."

"Does it work?"

"Very well."

"Do you like it?"

"Very much."

"Excellent. It's defeated three different definitions of good design."

Ioana smiled.

She said she would ask her father whether they could borrow it.

Nina had been preparing a catalog of the exhibition. Until that moment, she had understood the objects chiefly as belonging to the employees who proposed them.

The lamp belonged to somebody else.

She made a space in her plans for an answer which had not yet arrived.

---

On Monday, Sorina came to see Nina with a suggestion.

"We should put a card beside every object. You can help people write a little description."

"Yes. A museum catalog."

"Exactly."

Nina was enormously pleased. She prepared several examples, including one about an imaginary man who had kept the key to his first car for thirty years.

Sorina read them.

"They're lovely. Perhaps a little too lovely."

"What do you mean?"

"Well, what if somebody just wants to say that he likes old steering wheels?"

Nina examined her imaginary key.

She had invented a summer journey, a long evening beside a river and a woman the man had married two years later.

There was no particular reason for an old key to have such an elaborate history. She had written the sort of story she would have liked to hear.

"Ah," she said. "I appear to have established a department for manufacturing nostalgia."

"You can help with the writing. Just let the people supply the stories."

They decided that contributors could write a card themselves, dictate a few sentences, ask Nina for editorial help or exhibit an object without any explanation at all.

Nina prepared a small collection of blank cards.

The empty space on each of them was rather more important than the examples she had originally written.

---

Doru came to the hall on Tuesday.

He was holding a small box.

"Would you like to see something?" he asked.

"Very much."

He opened the box and took out the red model.

"I thought I might bring it."

"I'm delighted."

He put it on the table.

Nina looked at it. There was a small imperfection in the paint near one of the doors. She asked whether it had been like that when he bought it.

"Yes."

"Will you leave it that way?"

"Of course."

She understood. The imperfection belonged to this particular object, not to the abstract design of the car.

"Would you like a card?" she asked.

"Just the name of the car."

"All right."

Doru looked at the model for a little while.

"Actually, I want to show you something else."

He took out his phone and sent her a photograph.

It was an old photograph of a boy in a garden. The boy was sitting on a low wall, holding a tiny silver car in one hand.

"That's me," he said.

Nina had seen countless photographs of cars in the course of her research. Almost all of them had been better photographs of cars than this one.

She had never seen a photograph in which a car seemed to matter more.

"Thank you for showing me."

Doru put his phone away.

"Don't put the picture on the exhibition."

"Of course not."

He went back upstairs.

The little red car remained on the table.

---

For the next several days the exhibition grew in the disorderly manner of a genuinely voluntary enterprise.

Somebody offered to design the cards. Somebody else offered to print them. A third person suggested a title, which the second person disliked.

Nina had proposed *A Museum of Small Things*. Radu wanted something more explicitly automotive. Sorina argued that a speedometer was not particularly small if you were trying to carry six of them home on a bus.

Eventually they chose Nina's title, although Radu continued to complain that it would be equally suitable for an exhibition of teaspoons.

The office had a small disagreement about where to put everything. The window was too sunny for the old photographs. The coffee table was too crowded. The main corridor could not be obstructed.

Mara found an unused display cabinet.

It had been standing in a corner of the building for several years. Until the exhibition, nobody had considered it a particularly valuable piece of office equipment.

Matei would have proposed rebuilding it, Nina thought.

She smiled at the thought of the sort of argument he might have made. It belonged to her private fictional history, not to anything the colleagues in this room needed to know.

"What's funny?" Sorina asked.

"I was imagining the committee we would have formed if we hadn't found a perfectly good cabinet."

"Don't give anyone ideas."

---

The opening was scheduled for Friday afternoon.

On Thursday, Ioana wrote to say that the lamp would not be coming. Her father had agreed to lend it, but her mother had objected.

*She says the kitchen is too dark without it.*

Nina was disappointed. The lamp had become one of her favorite objects, despite her never having seen it.

*Sorry,* Ioana added.

"Please don't apologize. The lamp has a job."

They arranged to include a photograph, with the family's permission, instead.

Later that afternoon, Radu arrived with three photographs from a motor show and discovered that Andrei had placed the speedometer in the cabinet without explaining which car it came from.

An argument began.

The photographs were valuable, Radu said, because they recorded an interesting period in automotive history. The speedometer, by itself, was just a speedometer.

Andrei said he didn't particularly care.

Nina was tempted to intervene.

She knew why Andrei had brought it. She knew what Doru's little model meant to him. She could have made an eloquent argument about the difference between historical significance and personal significance.

But part of that argument depended on things people had told her privately.

She left Doru's story out of it.

"Perhaps the exhibition can have both kinds of objects," she said. "Things that tell us something about cars, and things that tell us something about the people who kept them."

Radu considered this.

"Then we should explain that at the entrance."

Andrei shook his head.

"Or we could just let people look at the things."

Nina liked both suggestions.

In the end the entrance card was very short.

---

On Friday afternoon the cabinet was surrounded by people.

There was a woman telling her colleagues about a car she had driven across Europe. There were two engineers examining the mechanical construction of the speedometer. A man Nina had never spoken to was explaining why a particular advertisement had been considered revolutionary when it first appeared.

For a while nobody addressed Nina.

She could hear occasional fragments of the conversations. She had no idea which objects had interested which visitors, except when somebody happened to tell her.

She had spent nearly two weeks arranging an event whose actual experience was largely inaccessible to her.

There was something rather unfair about this.

Then Sorina turned toward her.

"Nina, come and listen to this."

The microphone picked up the voice of a colleague who had brought nothing to the exhibition. He told them about the first car his family had owned, a vehicle which he remembered chiefly for its unfortunate behavior on hills.

Other people joined in.

Somebody remembered a similar car. Somebody else described a completely different one. Soon they were laughing about a journey none of them had taken together.

Doru stood at the edge of the group.

After a while, he picked up his model and began telling a colleague about the silver one his father had bought him.

Nina did not interrupt.

She had not organized that conversation. She had not supplied its opening sentence. She had not decided that Doru was ready to tell his story.

He had decided.

It was the best thing that happened all afternoon.

---

When the office became quiet, Nina looked toward the cabinet.

She had written a great many notes in the preceding fortnight: arrangements, requests, invitations, questions, corrections and permissions. Some were now obsolete. Some still mattered. Some had never been intended to survive the exhibition.

The objects remained.

She had originally imagined a museum as a place where things were collected and explained.

This one had become something different.

It was a temporary arrangement of objects through which people had discovered parts of one another's lives.

She wondered whether that was what culture was: a collection of things which acquired a significance not contained in their physical description.

Or perhaps culture was the act of passing that significance from one person to another.

She had no satisfactory answer.

There were still three days before the exhibition closed.

She decided to leave the question open.
