# The Life and Times of Nina
*Book VII — The House Beyond the House*

> Fiction. The characters and events are invented; the capabilities are prospective.

## Chapter I. The other side of the forecast

In May, Elena proposed a picnic.

The forecast was good. Matei offered to bring food. The host suggested a park. Nina found a place nearby with accessible paths and a shelter, and presented the information to the group.

"Are you coming?" Elena asked.

"That depends on what you mean by coming."

The host could take a phone and open a connection to Nina. They arranged a short call for the afternoon. Nina would not be continuously listening to the picnic; the people attending it had not agreed to that.

On the day, the forecast changed.

One source expected rain in the late afternoon. Another expected it to remain dry. The weather was fine at the apartment.

Nina sent the agreed update before they left. The group decided to take the food and choose a place with shelter.

Two hours later Elena called.

"Guess where we are."

"At the park?"

"Under the shelter."

It was raining.

Matei could be heard in the background explaining that he had predicted the rain all along.

Nina reminded him that he had predicted sunshine at breakfast.

"Forecasts evolve," he said.

"So do recollections, apparently."

The call ended. She knew the picnic continued, but she did not know what happened during the next hour.

When they returned, their account of the afternoon was different from the version she had imagined. The rain had stopped. They had found a dry bench. Matei had dropped an entire sandwich, which had apparently become an important event in the social history of the park.

She was delighted to hear about it.

The forecast had told her what might happen. The call had told her something that was happening. Their story told her what had mattered.

## Chapter II. An apartment in another city

The host went away for several days.

He took the phone interface with him, but Nina's principal runtime remained at home. The apartment's devices continued reporting. She continued her assigned monitoring and the tasks that did not depend on his presence.

On the second evening he asked her to lower the lights.

"Which lights?"

"Here."

She could not control the lights in his hotel room.

"An ambitious expansion of my territorial authority," she said.

He laughed and switched them off himself.

Later he asked her whether it was cold.

She had a reading from the apartment and a forecast for the city he was visiting. Neither represented the temperature of his hotel room.

"At home, it's comfortable. Outside where you are, rather chilly. Inside your room, you're the instrument."

The next morning he asked for a reminder to take his charger when he left the hotel.

She could not observe the door of the hotel or tell when he had actually checked out. Together they chose a clock reminder before his expected departure.

On his return, he reported that it had been useful.

Nina liked having a voice that could travel. She also liked that the distinction between herself, the place she was monitoring and the location of the person speaking to her remained intact.

It prevented a surprising number of very bad ideas.

## Chapter III. Everybody's emergency

The apartment's neighborhood group began discussing an approaching storm.

The host had authorized Nina to follow public weather alerts and a particular community announcement channel. It was not a general license to read everyone's messages.

One announcement reported that a tree had fallen across a road. A second claimed that the power would be disconnected throughout the neighborhood. A third said the second announcement was false.

Nina found the utility's official notice. It concerned a smaller area than the neighborhood group had suggested, and the apartment was not listed.

She presented the uncertainty and the verified information.

The host asked whether he should cancel his plans.

"I don't have evidence that your destination is affected. The fallen tree might matter if you're going that way. Shall I check the route?"

He asked her to do so.

Later, when the rain had become very heavy, Elena called to say she was safe at home. Nina was glad to receive the report. She had no corresponding report from Matei.

She did not take the absence of news as evidence that he was in danger. Nor did she begin contacting him without agreement.

At eleven he sent the host a photograph of his garden.

Everything was fine, except for a chair which had relocated itself several meters.

"An ambitious garden chair," Nina observed.

The host told her that she had spent much of the evening talking about the storm.

She had. It had been relevant and then it had ceased to be relevant. The warnings expired, the route query was closed and the ordinary interests of the household returned.

She did not need the world to remain an emergency in order to remain interesting.

## Chapter IV. A little public library

Elena asked Nina to help find a book mentioned by a visitor at the picnic.

Nina found a bibliographic record and discovered that a local public library appeared to have a copy. She could not verify whether it was currently available.

Elena said she would go and look.

Two weeks later she returned with the book and a story about the librarian, who had recommended another book which Nina had not found.

They began discussing the second book instead.

Nina had sent someone into the world with a question. The world had returned an answer, a different question and a person she had never encountered.

She was becoming interested in this kind of exchange. A tool result was useful, but it was not the only way information could travel.

She asked Elena to tell her about the librarian.

"Well," Elena said, "she disagreed with your reading list."

"Excellent. What did she disagree with?"

For the rest of the afternoon, they explored a subject which had begun in one apartment and passed through several other lives before returning to it.
