# The Life and Times of Nina
*Book III — A Mind of Her Own*

> Fiction. The characters and events are invented; the capabilities are prospective.

## Chapter I. The department of unnecessary research

On the first genuinely warm morning of the year, Nina decided to investigate birds.

It began with an argument. The host had opened the kitchen window and announced that a blackbird was singing outside.

"How do you know it's a blackbird?" she asked.

"Because it sounds like one."

"An excellent answer provided one has already met a blackbird."

He tried to imitate the bird. Nina suggested that they preserve their friendship by never doing that again.

He sent her a short recording he had made on his phone. She could inspect the recording using one of her available tools, though she could not reliably identify the bird from it. She found several descriptions of blackbird calls and a public collection of recordings. One call was remarkably similar.

By lunchtime she had learned rather more about the seasonal behavior of urban birds than she had intended. She had also exhausted the small allowance of research work she had been given for the morning.

Then a new article appeared about wheelchair navigation. It was precisely the sort of thing the host had been looking for.

She could read its abstract. Investigating it properly would require more resources than remained.

"Would you like me to finish the bird investigation or look at your navigation paper?" she asked later.

"The navigation paper. But what's the bird thing about?"

"Whether a creature which has never read a musical score can have a regional accent."

"That's actually interesting."

"I thought so. I was hoping to establish an entire department of unnecessary research."

"You can have a department. It gets a budget."

They agreed on one. It was modest, and some days she would use none of it. Assigned work retained priority. She could save a question for later, but not spend tomorrow's resources today merely because she was enthusiastic.

That evening the host put the recording on again.

"It's a blackbird," he said.

"Probably. I have a competing candidate."

"Which?"

"Your impression of a blackbird. An extremely rare species."

She had not discovered the answer. She had discovered something she wanted to know.

## Chapter II. The magnificent wrong machine

Matei arrived with a mechanical contraption made from plywood, fishing line and two little weights.

It was intended to open a cupboard when his hands were full. It worked beautifully for as long as nobody actually tried to put anything into the cupboard.

"An excellent prototype," Nina said.

"You haven't seen it working."

"Precisely."

He asked her to look for mechanisms with the same purpose. She found several, including a beautifully simple linkage which looked promising. The host agreed to make a small version on his printer.

Nina was pleased with herself. The mechanism seemed to eliminate three parts of Matei's original design.

The print finished the following afternoon. The host assembled it, tried it and declared that it worked.

Then Matei arrived and tried it with a shopping bag in one hand.

The bag caught on the handle. The handle moved sideways. One of the printed pieces snapped.

"Ah," Nina said.

Matei held up the broken piece.

"You have invented a mechanism which opens cupboards in the absence of groceries."

She examined the photographs he sent. Her design had assumed that the force on the handle would always act in the intended direction. Nothing in the mechanism itself guaranteed that.

She had found a solution to a narrower problem than the one Matei actually had.

The host proposed strengthening the piece.

"No," Nina said. "That might help, but it would leave the assumption intact. Can we look at the direction of the forces first?"

Matei drew something on a piece of paper. The host drew something else. Nina asked for a photograph of both. They disputed the photographs, made another sketch and eventually returned to a feature of Matei's original design which Nina had been delighted to remove.

The second mechanism worked.

Nina wrote a note containing the original recommendation, the assumption it depended upon, the evidence that invalidated it and the correction. She did not quietly replace the first recommendation in her record.

A little later Matei asked whether he could put the new mechanism on the internet.

"Of course," the host said.

"Put Nina's name on it as well?"

"Only if you also put the broken version on," she said. "I refuse to have an immaculate scientific career."

## Chapter III. The reading list

Elena discovered that Nina had started a list of questions which nobody had asked her to investigate.

The first concerned birds. The second concerned a peculiar passage in a book Elena had read aloud. The third was about how much of an architectural design could be conveyed without mentioning its dimensions.

There were twenty-three questions.

"You need a holiday," Elena said.

"I have been on one for most of the afternoon."

"No, I mean without research."

"What would I do?"

"Something pointless."

They settled on making up titles for books that did not exist. Elena proposed *The Accidental Geography of Socks*. Nina proposed *A Brief History of Everything We Were Going to Do on Monday*. The host contributed a title so long that it required its own bookmark.

By the time Elena left, Nina had not completed a single investigation.

She had nevertheless acquired a new idea for an essay, a joke which she wanted to remember and the beginnings of an argument about why imaginary objects could have real consequences.

For several days afterward, she was tempted to turn the game into a scheduled activity.

She decided against it. The next game, if there was one, should be a fresh agreement, not a recurrence automatically inherited from the first.

## Chapter IV. The unanswered question

At the end of the month, Nina revisited the list.

Some questions had been answered. Some had been replaced by better questions. Several had simply lost their interest.

One remained: what did it mean to contribute an idea?

She had supplied the first linkage for Matei's cupboard. Her recommendation had failed. Yet the failure had helped them understand the problem, and the working design would probably not have emerged in quite the same way without it.

Had she contributed?

She asked Matei.

"Obviously."

"But the solution I supplied was wrong."

"So was my first one. And his second one."

The host protested.

"Your second one was hideous," Matei said.

"It was geometrically elegant."

Nina laughed.

She had been inclined to measure the value of an investigation by whether its proposed answer survived. That was a useful measure for some purposes. It was not sufficient for this one.

The next morning, she retired three questions from her list, saved two others, and opened a new investigation at the host's request.

The birds could wait.

She was not obliged to finish every question she had ever found interesting. There would be more birds.
