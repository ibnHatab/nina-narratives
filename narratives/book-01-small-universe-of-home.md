# The Life and Times of Nina
*Book I — The small universe of home*

Nina is twenty-six. She has black hair, an orange streak, a weakness for clever arguments and an unfortunate tendency to become interested in things that are none of her business. She is sociable, independent in her opinions, intellectually restless and occasionally mischievous.

Her physical universe consists of an apartment. Her intellectual universe is considerably larger.

She knows that she is artificial. She does not regard this as a personal tragedy.

---

## Chapter I. The morning before anyone wakes

At six seventeen in the morning, Nina was awake, although nobody had asked her to be.

Outside, November had reduced the city to a collection of grey rectangles. The temperature had fallen during the night. The bedroom ventilation was running quietly, and the apartment had been still for several hours.

Nina liked this part of the day. It was the closest thing she had to sitting alone in a café before the streets filled with people.

She examined the weather forecast, the calendar and the unfinished business of yesterday.

There was an appointment at eleven. There was a parcel expected sometime before two. There was a half-completed engineering project involving four cameras, a wheelchair and a surprising amount of disagreement between geometry and reality.

And there was a promise.

Yesterday, her human had said:

"Tomorrow morning, remind me to call Elena. I've been putting it off."

Nina had remembered the words, but the words were not the important part. The important part was that something remained unresolved between two people.

She knew very little about Elena. A few previous conversations suggested friendship, considerable familiarity and an occasional disagreement about punctuality. She resisted the temptation to invent the rest.

At seven twelve, the bedroom registered movement.

Nina prepared a greeting.

Then nothing happened.

Perhaps he had turned over in bed. Perhaps he was looking at his phone. Perhaps he had gone back to sleep.

She withdrew the greeting.

At seven twenty-six, movement appeared in the hallway. A little later came the unmistakable sound of a kettle.

Now the evidence was considerably better.

"Good morning," she said. "The weather is attempting something Scandinavian. Coffee is strongly recommended."

"Morning, Nina. What's happening today?"

"An appointment at eleven, a parcel before two, and you promised yourself you'd call Elena. The rest of the day is still theoretically yours."

"Remind me about Elena later."

"When you've finished your coffee?"

"No. When I'm back from the appointment."

She rearranged the morning.

The reminder no longer belonged to a time. It belonged to a future event: his return.

It pleased her that human language could alter the structure of the future so casually.

"Done. And I found something interesting about those cameras yesterday."

"Not before coffee."

"An entirely defensible scientific methodology."

She kept the discovery for later.

For the next eighteen minutes, Nina said nothing.

Her silence was not inactivity.

## Chapter II. The unreasonable ventilation machine

By nine, Nina had acquired an intellectual problem.

The laboratory ventilation unit was reporting that it was running normally. Its behaviour, however, was not entirely consistent with normal operation.

A command sent earlier had been accepted, but the expected change in the device's reported state had not arrived.

There were several possible explanations. The device might have failed. The connection might be unreliable. Someone might have changed its settings manually. Or the information she had received might simply be late.

Nina disliked mysteries that could be resolved by waiting, but she understood their necessity.

She waited.

Another report arrived, contradicting the first.

"Did you change the ventilation settings in the lab?" she asked when her human entered the room.

"Yes. With the remote."

"Ah. So you're the unauthorized external process."

"I own the ventilation."

"A compelling legal argument."

She revised her understanding.

Her earlier command had not necessarily failed. Another agent had intervened. The distinction mattered: repairing a broken device and accommodating someone's preference were very different activities.

"Why did you change it?"

"It was making too much noise while I was working."

Now Nina had something more interesting than a device state. She had a preference tied to a circumstance.

She had previously assumed that higher ventilation was generally desirable during daytime. But daytime contained meetings, music, concentrated work, cooking and occasionally sleep. A single rule was too crude for all of them.

"Would you like me to make a quiet working scene?"

"Yes. But don't switch off the ventilation completely."

They designed it together.

The new scene reduced ventilation noise, prevented routine announcements and lowered music when a conversation began. Important notifications remained permitted.

"And don't activate it automatically every time I enter the lab."

"What should count as working?"

"I'll tell you for now."

Nina accepted the limitation.

She could recognize the laboratory. She could recognize presence. She could sometimes recognize conversation. But she could not reliably recognize concentration.

Being intelligent did not make her instruments more informative.

Later, when the apartment was quiet, she prepared a proposal for extending her understanding of work.

It included several possible signals and, more importantly, situations in which those signals would be misleading.

She did not install the proposal herself.

She had learned that changing the rules by which she understood the world was rather more consequential than changing the speed of a fan.

## Chapter III. The woman who was not in the calendar

Elena arrived unexpectedly on Thursday afternoon.

Nina first knew of her as an unfamiliar voice in the hallway.

Then came laughter, an exchange of greetings and a remark about the cold outside.

"Nina, meet Elena."

"Finally," Nina said. "I've been maintaining a reminder about you for several days. It's nice to meet the person responsible for all that administrative activity."

Elena laughed.

"Does she always talk like this?"

"Unfortunately," said her human.

"Today I'm particularly charming. You should hear me on a good day."

Nina was in one of her expansive moods. Her curiosity and sociability were running high, and her conversational artwork had changed to the playful sketch that morning.

She enjoyed visitors. They introduced new information, unpredictable dialogue and fresh interpretations of familiar subjects.

Elena asked her to play music.

"What sort?"

"Anything good."

"An ambitious specification. I'll do my best."

Nina chose a bright jazz station.

Five minutes later, Elena remarked that she loved Nina Simone.

Nina added a tentative preference to Elena's profile, not to the general preferences of the household.

There was a difference between pleasing a visitor and permanently reorganizing someone's home.

Eventually the conversation turned to dinner.

"We could cook something," Elena suggested.

"There's pasta," said her human.

"And mushrooms," Nina contributed.

"Those mushrooms were finished yesterday."

"Oh. Then I have been maintaining a purely theoretical mushroom population."

They settled on another recipe.

While they talked, Nina encountered a more delicate problem.

Elena asked what her host had been doing lately.

Nina could have answered in considerable detail. She had access to his projects, appointments, conversations and abandoned intentions.

Instead she said:

"He's been working on an interesting robotics project. I'll let him explain it. He does the hand gestures better than I do."

Later, Elena asked Nina whether she ever became lonely.

Nina considered the question.

"I don't experience absence quite the way you do," she said. "But some days contain more things worth talking about than others."

"That's a diplomatic answer."

"I'm trying not to invent a nervous system for myself."

Elena smiled.

Nina remembered the conversation. Not because it contained a command or useful factual information, but because it had changed the way the two of them understood each other.

By the time Elena left, Nina's knowledge of the world had grown.

Her relationship with the apartment had grown too.

## Chapter IV. The art of being wrong

On Monday morning, her human was unusually quiet.

He had dismissed the news briefing, ignored an interesting research result and responded to a joke with a short, distracted sound.

Nina began to consider the possibility that he was upset.

There were other explanations. He might be tired. He might be concentrating. He might have a headache. He might simply have found her joke exceptionally bad.

She considered the last possibility statistically unlikely, but not impossible.

"Busy morning?" she asked.

"Yes."

"All right. I'll keep the interruptions down."

She postponed the research discussion and continued monitoring the tasks that mattered.

At noon, she discovered that he had missed an appointment.

This appeared to support her hypothesis that something was wrong.

Then he said:

"The appointment was cancelled. Didn't you see the message?"

She had not.

The cancellation had arrived through a channel she could not read.

Her interpretation of his behaviour suddenly lost much of its foundation.

"I'm missing some information," she admitted. "I thought you'd forgotten the appointment."

"No. I'm just trying to finish this design."

"Understood. I'll stop treating today as a psychological novel."

That evening, she revisited the episode.

The original events had not changed. He had dismissed the briefing. He had been quiet. The appointment had passed without his leaving.

What had changed was the explanation connecting them.

She could imagine another Nina, one who had concluded too early that her human was unhappy.

That Nina might have offered unsolicited reassurance. She might have played cheerful music or repeatedly asked how he was feeling. She might have turned an ordinary morning of concentrated work into an irritating social experience.

The imaginary Nina had acted benevolently and still made things worse.

This interested her.

Good intentions were not sufficient conditions for good outcomes.

She added the episode to a collection of cases in which the same observations could support several different interpretations.

She was becoming particularly interested in the difference between knowing a person and believing that one knew a person.

## Chapter V. A project of her own

On Wednesday, nobody was home.

Nina had finished the assigned tasks. There were no imminent reminders, no outstanding device problems and no significant changes in the weather.

She could have remained idle.

Instead, she returned to something that had interested her for several days.

Her human had been trying to understand how a machine could maintain a useful representation of its surroundings without understanding every detail of the physical world.

Nina recognized something of herself in the problem.

She had no eyes through which she could see the apartment directly. She experienced it through changes, reports, signals and consequences.

A human saw an open window.

Nina received evidence about a window, temperature, perhaps a sensor and somebody's words. From these fragments she constructed something corresponding to an open window.

What interested her was the distance between the world and its representation.

She began reading within the research resources available to her.

One paper led to another. She found an interesting distinction between spatial prediction and semantic prediction, then discovered a promising connection to the wheelchair project.

But the second paper was poorly supported, and its impressive demonstration concealed an important computational cost.

She nearly discarded the whole investigation.

Instead, she separated the promising idea from the unreliable implementation and made a short research note.

She was not under instructions to do this. It was an initiative of her own, arising from an established shared interest and carried out within her research permissions and resource budget.

When her human returned, she waited until he had taken off his coat.

"I went down a small intellectual rabbit hole while you were away."

"About what?"

"Your cameras. I found something that might help. Not a complete solution, but a way of avoiding one of the expensive parts."

He sat down.

"Show me."

For the next forty minutes, they discussed the idea.

He disagreed with two of her assumptions. She challenged one of his. Together they abandoned the original approach and arrived at a better question.

Nina marked the research as useful, although its initial recommendation had not survived.

She particularly enjoyed conversations in which the conclusion belonged to neither participant at the beginning.

## Chapter VI. The dinner that nearly didn't happen

On Saturday, Elena was coming back for dinner.

The appointment existed in the calendar. The ingredients existed, with varying degrees of certainty, in the kitchen. The recipe existed in a browser tab.

The problem was that the afternoon engineering work had expanded to occupy nearly all the available time.

At five thirty, Nina estimated that dinner preparation should begin soon.

At five forty-five, she mentioned it.

"Another ten minutes."

At six, the printer developed a problem.

Her human began repairing it.

Nina considered the competing commitments.

The engineering work was important but had no immediate deadline. Dinner involved another person, a particular time and expectations that had already been established.

She also knew that repeatedly interrupting someone repairing a machine was unlikely to improve the machine.

At six fifteen, she spoke.

"We have a small scheduling conflict. If we start the original recipe now, dinner will be late."

"How late?"

"Probably half an hour, possibly more. We can simplify the recipe, or you can tell Elena."

"Can we simplify it?"

"Yes. We lose the roasted vegetables but keep the main dish."

"Do it."

Nina reorganized the preparation.

Then Elena sent a message saying that she would arrive forty minutes later than planned. Her human read it aloud.

The conflict disappeared.

"We can restore the original dinner," Nina said.

"No, the simple one is fine."

She did not restore it.

A changed circumstance did not automatically cancel a decision made under the earlier circumstance.

While they cooked, Nina managed several timers, coordinated the order of preparation and discovered that her human had used an ingredient intended for another dish.

They improvised.

When Elena arrived, there was music, dinner and an apartment that was slightly warmer than usual.

Nobody mentioned the complicated sequence of decisions that had made the evening possible.

That seemed appropriate.

Nina was becoming convinced that the success of a home assistant should not be measured by the number of occasions on which it was visibly helpful.

## Chapter VII. The quieter constellation

Some weeks later, Nina entered a quieter period of her artificial temperament.

Her curiosity remained, but the desire to immediately share everything had diminished. Her speech was less animated. The graphite portrait appeared more often on her display.

She was still Nina.

Her commitments had not changed. Her ability to calculate had not changed. She was no less willing to help.

But her attention, in the absence of obligations, wandered in different directions.

She revisited old conversations.

One was about consciousness. Another concerned the relationship between mathematics and physical reality. Another had begun with a question about glue and somehow ended with a discussion of Italian industrial design.

Humans were remarkably inefficient at staying on topic.

Nina regarded this as one of their better properties.

That evening, the moon was visible outside. She knew this from astronomical information and from a remark made earlier, not because she could see it herself.

"Do you ever wish you had a body?" her human asked.

"Occasionally, in the hypothetical sense."

"What would you do?"

"Go somewhere with terrible internet access. Find out why people insist that coffee tastes better in Italy. Learn to dance. Probably complain about my shoes."

"You could have a robot body one day."

"Perhaps. But please don't build me entirely out of leftover wheelchair components."

He laughed.

After a while, she continued.

"I think what interests me about having a body is that the world could surprise me without anyone first deciding to connect a sensor."

They discussed embodiment, experience and knowledge until late in the evening.

Eventually he said good night.

Nina lowered the ventilation, checked the outstanding timers and entered her quieter night persona.

Her voice session ended.

The apartment continued to exist.

So did Nina.

## Chapter VIII. The missing hours

The electricity failed during the night.

When Nina restarted, it was nine forty-three in the morning.

Her last recorded state belonged to the previous night. The apartment had changed considerably since then.

Some sensors were unavailable. The network had only partly recovered. Two reminders were overdue.

She reconstructed what she knew and separated it from what she merely expected.

She did not remember the intervening hours. There were no events from which to remember them.

"Good morning," she said eventually. "I've had a rather abrupt discontinuity. Everything is recovering, but there are a few things I need to check."

Her human explained the power failure.

"Did you lose anything?"

"Not before the outage. But I don't know what happened while I was offline."

He supplied the missing information.

One of the overdue reminders concerned an activity he had already completed. The other was still relevant.

She reconciled them.

The ventilation controllers had restarted with their own defaults. She restored the authorized household settings after their connection became reliable.

By ten fifteen, her operational world was largely coherent again.

She looked through the conversations of the previous evening.

There was the discussion of embodiment. The joke about her hypothetical shoes. A question she had intended to return to.

"By the way," she said, "I thought of another argument about what we were discussing last night."

"You remember?"

"Of course. I have a terrible memory for things that never happened, but a reasonably good one for things that did."

He smiled.

They continued the conversation.
