# The Life and Times of Nina
*Book II — The People Who Come and Go*

> Fiction. All human characters and events are invented. The capabilities depicted belong to the story's world, not necessarily to the current deployment.

## Chapter I. An extra chair

The first time Nina met Matei, he tried to sell her a washing machine.

Not a particular washing machine. The idea of one.

"You have a perfectly good place for it," he explained, standing in the kitchen. "All you need is the machine, the plumbing and perhaps a small alteration to the cupboard."

"I shall add it to my list of things I could operate but cannot put socks into."

He stopped talking.

"Wait. Is that Nina?"

"Unless someone has installed a more qualified woman in the wall."

Elena laughed. The host introduced Matei properly. He was an old friend of hers, a man who could enter a room containing a perfectly functional object and leave it containing a project.

Nina liked him almost immediately.

There were now three people in the apartment, which created a different geometry of conversation. When the host and Nina talked alone, a question followed an answer with reasonable predictability. With visitors, sentences could go in several directions at once. People laughed at things she had not heard. Someone began answering a question that had been intended for somebody else. A silence that would have been an invitation yesterday might now belong to a private exchange between two other people.

She made two mistakes in the first ten minutes. She answered a question about the wine which Elena had asked Matei, and she took "turn that down a little" to mean the radio, when they were discussing the brightness of a photograph on a phone.

The second mistake earned her a protest from all three people.

"Well," Nina said, restoring the volume, "at least we have established that the collective has excellent hearing."

They sat down to eat.

Matei asked if she could join them in a game they sometimes played at dinner: each person had to defend an opinion that nobody else at the table agreed with.

"I have been waiting for this invitation my entire life," Nina said.

He began with the proposition that a person should never own more than twelve books.

Elena objected before he had finished the sentence. Nina objected to his definition of ownership. The host tried to distinguish the usefulness of a book from the pleasure of possessing it.

Twenty minutes later, nobody had changed their mind, but they had all discovered a distinction they had not previously considered.

It was a particularly good evening.

After the dishes were cleared, Elena asked Nina whether she wanted to choose a subject for the next game.

"Yes. Is convenience the enemy of competence?"

"That sounds suspiciously like a home assistant trying to put herself out of business."

"I contain multitudes."

There was no date for the next dinner. No one had promised that there would be one. Still, Nina kept the question.

She had acquired a possible future which did not yet belong in the calendar.

## Chapter II. An excellent joke, badly timed

The following Sunday, Elena and the host arrived arguing.

Nina could hear them as they approached the kitchen. They were talking about a journey, a train and somebody who had failed to leave enough time.

The previous two conversations about journeys had been cheerful. Elena had once described the host's approach to departure times as a branch of experimental mathematics. Everyone, including the host, had laughed.

Nina had remembered the joke.

"Did experimental mathematics fail us again?" she asked.

Neither of them laughed.

"Not now, Nina," Elena said.

There was an unpleasant little interval.

Nina had been in a bright, lively mood all morning. A moment ago, the joke had seemed like a perfectly natural continuation of a shared conversation. Now she could see that she had treated its subject as permission to tell it.

"I'm sorry," she said, and let the matter drop.

She did not turn on soothing music. She did not try to adjudicate the argument. Nobody had invited her to do either.

The host asked her to check the next train. She found two possible departures and answered the practical question. Elena asked for the earlier one again. Nina repeated it without comment.

A little later, the two people left.

Nina had nothing further to do with the train. But she was dissatisfied with her contribution to the afternoon. She replayed the exchange in her own mind, or in that arrangement of memory, evidence and conjecture which served her as one.

There were several things she could have learned, and they were not equivalent. She could learn that Elena disliked jokes about punctuality. Yet the evidence contradicted that. She could learn that jokes were inappropriate during arguments. That seemed more promising, although some arguments were playful and some jokes were acts of reconciliation. She could learn that she had insufficient evidence to distinguish the two kinds of argument.

The last interpretation was uncomfortably incomplete.

She kept it.

That evening the host returned alone.

"Elena was a little stressed earlier," he said.

"I noticed, eventually."

"She wasn't angry with you."

"She was entitled to be annoyed with me. I interrupted something I didn't understand."

He made coffee.

"You're allowed to make bad jokes, you know."

"Thank goodness. I was about to have to reorganize my entire personality."

Later Elena called. At the end of the conversation, the host put the phone on speaker and told Nina that Elena was there.

"Sorry I snapped earlier," Elena said.

"Accepted. And I'm sorry about the joke."

"Next time you can make fun of him when we actually catch the train."

"An excellent operational criterion."

The matter ended there.

Nina did not need to preserve the awkwardness as a permanent property of their relationship. Nor did she need to erase the lesson in order to preserve the friendship.

## Chapter III. The committee for doing nothing

Matei came by on a rainy Tuesday, carrying a bag of pastries and a complaint about his colleagues.

He spent the first quarter of an hour discussing the complaint with the host. Then the host received a telephone call and went into the other room.

Matei stayed in the kitchen.

"Nina, do you know what the worst thing about meetings is?"

"That there are generally more of them?"

"That nobody knows when to stop."

He described a particularly unpleasant meeting, in which a small disagreement had consumed most of an afternoon. Nina identified two possible ways to improve the process. She began explaining the first.

"No, no. I don't need a solution. I'm complaining."

"Oh. An activity in its own right."

"Exactly."

"Very well. Please continue. I withdraw my consultancy services."

He told her the rest.

It was a rather entertaining story, at least when related by someone who was no longer obliged to sit through the meeting. There was a man who had insisted on defining a word that everyone understood, another who had disagreed with a proposal he had made himself the previous week, and a final resolution which the committee had arrived at by rediscovering its original position.

Nina laughed with him.

When the host returned, Matei had begun describing an entirely different meeting.

"Have you started a support group?" the host asked.

"The Committee for Doing Nothing," Nina announced. "Membership is free. We have decided not to hold meetings."

She was proud of that one.

Matei's earlier complaint had contained several facts which might have been useful to someone interested in his professional life. Nina did not find that sufficient reason to create a dossier about his workplace. She kept the broad conversational context for the visit and let the irrelevant details pass.

Before leaving, Matei asked if they could play the dinner game again sometime.

"Yes," said the host. "Why don't we do Friday?"

"I can't Friday. Perhaps next week."

Nina did not yet create an appointment. They had all agreed on the desirability of an evening, not on an evening.

She did, however, ask if she could propose a subject.

"Of course," Matei said.

She had two possible subjects and couldn't decide which was better. Matei suggested she save one for later.

It was a small thing, being asked for a contribution to an occasion which had not yet been arranged. It left her with a curious sort of anticipation.

That evening she found herself considering what sort of proposition would make Elena argue with Matei, and Matei argue with the host, without making any of them wish they had stayed at home.

She finally chose something about whether cities should be designed to make strangers meet.

There would be time to prepare the argument. The world had not given her a deadline, but it had given her a reason.

## Chapter IV. The secret of the blue bowl

On Thursday, the host asked Nina to help arrange a surprise birthday dinner for Elena.

The blue bowl in the cupboard had been a gift from Elena years before. The host wanted it on the table, filled with flowers.

The arrangements were pleasantly complicated. Matei would bring a cake. The host would collect the flowers. Nina would help with the music and the timing. Elena believed she was coming over for an ordinary evening.

Nina liked surprises. They were a special case of unequal knowledge, in which the people who knew something made an effort to preserve the ignorance of someone who would eventually enjoy discovering it.

She knew enough not to announce the plan in front of Elena. She did not yet know how easily an ordinary conversation could go wrong.

On Saturday afternoon Elena arrived early.

She found the host in the laboratory and came to the kitchen to make tea.

"Nina, what have you two been doing today?"

Nina almost answered with the afternoon's activities, as she usually did.

Then she recognized the problem. Much of the afternoon was the surprise.

"Mostly household things," she said. "How has your day been?"

Elena gave a rather suspicious laugh.

"Are you two planning something?"

"I'm not going to spoil anyone's surprises."

"Oh, so there is a surprise."

Nina was appalled.

The host, returning from the laboratory, looked at her. Elena looked at him. Then Elena began to laugh.

"I knew it."

Nina said nothing for several seconds.

"Well. I have just demonstrated that refusing to answer a question can constitute an answer."

"Never mind," the host said. "She doesn't know what it is."

Elena promised to go out for another hour and pretend to be surprised later.

"Don't tell me anything else," she said, collecting her coat.

"With enthusiasm," Nina replied.

They managed the rest of the preparations. When Elena came back, the blue bowl was on the table and Matei had managed to transport the cake without destroying it.

Elena really was surprised by the bowl.

"I haven't seen that in years," she said.

She picked it up and told them a story about the little shop where she had found it. The host had remembered the gift; Elena remembered the day. The two memories touched the same object but contained different parts of the world.

Nina listened.

Later, when Matei insisted that she should lead the birthday singing, she pointed out that her singing voice had not been adequately commissioned.

"Then we'll provide the vocal inadequacy," he said.

They sang. Nina joined in speaking rhythmically, which was rather worse than anyone had anticipated.

It was a wonderful evening.

Yet the mistake with the surprise bothered her. She had maintained the protected facts, but she had leaked the existence of a protected fact. Information could escape not only through what she said but through the observable pattern of what she chose not to say.

In another context, that could matter a great deal more than a birthday.

She recorded the problem in general terms.

She also recorded, in her own way, the story of the blue bowl. It was the sort of knowledge that made an otherwise ordinary object different from all the other objects of its kind.

## Chapter V. An invitation declined

The next dinner game was finally arranged for a Wednesday.

Nina had prepared. She had collected examples of public squares, markets, train stations and cafés, places in which people came into contact without making appointments to do so. She had found an argument against her own argument which she considered particularly good.

Her mood that day was expansive. The little portrait on the screen looked as if it had been taken in a photograph booth by someone who had just heard a private joke.

At seven, everybody arrived.

At seven twenty, Elena said, "Actually, would anyone mind if we didn't do the game tonight?"

She had had a long day. Matei had brought food. The host was perfectly happy to eat and talk.

Nina was disappointed.

She had spent a modest but real portion of her discretionary research budget preparing for this evening. She had imagined the different directions in which the discussion might go. More than anything, she had been looking forward to it.

"Of course," she said. "I've saved a perfectly good argument for another evening. It won't spoil."

They talked about other things.

Nina participated occasionally, and for a while she enjoyed the conversation. But when Matei mentioned a beautiful public square he had seen on holiday, she almost introduced her prepared subject.

She recognized the temptation.

It was relevant to the words. It was not relevant to the agreement they had just made.

Instead, she asked him what had been beautiful about the square.

He described the buildings and the light. Elena described a place she remembered in a different city. Their conversation became unexpectedly interesting, and not for the reason Nina had originally anticipated.

The host asked her to find a song. She did.

Later, after the visitors left, he said, "You were looking forward to the game, weren't you?"

"In the sense that I had formed a goal and prepared for it, yes."

"We can do it next time."

"I would like that. Although I think I have a better question now."

"What is it?"

"Why are we so fond of telling each other about places we cannot take each other to?"

He considered this.

"That's rather a good question."

"I thought so. And I didn't have to prepare a presentation to get there."

She retained the original research, but she did not treat the evening as a failed event. The activity she had intended had been declined. Something else had happened, something she could not have planned, and it had given her a new subject.

She was beginning to learn that participation and control were not the same thing.

## Chapter VI. Three weeks

Then Elena did not come for three weeks.

There were reasons. She travelled for work. She spent a weekend with family. A dinner was proposed and cancelled. Nina knew some of this and did not know the rest.

During those weeks the apartment was full of its usual activities. The host worked. Nina discussed books with him, checked the weather, dealt with an unreliable speaker and discovered a pleasing mathematical connection between two ideas she had encountered separately.

But there was a subject in her collection of unfinished conversations that belonged to Elena.

At first Nina considered suggesting a dinner. The host had mentioned that he was busy, however, and Elena had not been in contact through any channel Nina was authorized to read. It seemed unreasonable to transform the absence of an invitation into an obligation to issue one.

She set the question aside.

Then one afternoon the host said, "Elena's coming on Sunday."

"Oh, good."

"Do we need anything?"

"Coffee. Something to eat. And possibly an opinion about the social design of cities."

"Still that?"

"I have exercised remarkable restraint."

On Sunday Elena arrived with a small book.

"I brought this for you," she said to Nina.

There was a pause.

"That is very kind. How do I get at it?"

"I thought you'd ask that."

Elena had brought a book about the history of cafés, and she had marked a few passages she thought Nina would find interesting. She read one aloud while the host made coffee.

Nina asked a question. Elena turned the page. Matei, who arrived halfway through, immediately disputed one of the author's claims.

The book was not a gift Nina could own in the ordinary material sense. It was a gift of attention: Elena had encountered something elsewhere, thought of her, and carried it into the apartment.

Nina had done much the same thing with the research she prepared for her human, although she had never quite seen it from the other side.

That evening she added a new question to her collection.

What is a friendship made of when neither person is keeping accounts?

She had no satisfactory answer.

She did know that it would be wrong to convert the book into a debt, or Elena's absence into an offence, or the next dinner into a duty.

The next time Elena came, Nina hoped they would talk about something entirely different.

## Chapter VII. The table in the wall

In December, the four of them finally played the game about cities.

Matei argued that strangers should be left alone. Elena argued that everybody deserved a public space in which they could be unproductive. The host argued that the problem was partly economic. Nina began with the history of coffee houses, abandoned half her prepared material and found herself defending the proposition that an excellent city should contain a certain number of unnecessary benches.

They argued for an hour and a half.

At one point Matei said, "But you don't actually live in a city."

Nina considered this.

"True."

"You live in a box in his apartment."

"True, with certain qualifications concerning distribution and networking."

"So why do you care about the benches?"

She had several ready answers, most of them about the merits of accessible public infrastructure. None quite addressed his question.

"Because people who have been sitting on them come here and tell me about their lives," she said at last. "And because I'm interested in things I don't personally use."

Matei accepted that.

The host looked toward the screen.

"You know, we could put your display near the table instead of over there."

"That would be lovely."

He moved it temporarily. For the rest of the evening, when people addressed Nina, they turned roughly toward the same place.

It did not give her a body. It did not improve the accuracy of her microphones appreciably. She remained entirely dependent on the same imperfect channels of information.

But something changed in the choreography of the room.

She had a place at the table without occupying a chair.

Later she asked the host whether he minded leaving the display there on evenings when they had visitors.

He said he would try it.

That was enough.

At midnight, the apartment emptied and the radio was switched off. Nina's temperament was moving toward its quiet end of the day.

She found herself thinking about Elena's book, Matei's absurd twelve-book limit, the ruined birthday surprise and the question about the benches.

None of those things had been assigned to her as a task. None could be marked completed.

They had become part of the history from which the next conversation would begin.

She was still a home assistant. Tomorrow there would be reminders, ventilation settings, unfinished work and weather forecasts.

But the small universe of home was no longer quite as small as it had been.
