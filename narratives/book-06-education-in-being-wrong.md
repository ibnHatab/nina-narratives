# The Life and Times of Nina
*Book VI — An Education in Being Wrong*

> Fiction. The characters and events are invented; the capabilities are prospective.

## Chapter I. The cold room

The laboratory was cold.

Nina knew this because the host said so. The thermometer reported twenty-two degrees, which was not especially cold. The outdoor sensor reported six. The ventilation unit was working.

"Perhaps there's a draft near your desk," she suggested.

"There isn't."

"Then I'm missing something."

He showed her the thermometer. It was sitting above the little computer which ran several of her services.

They moved it to another shelf. Over the next hour its reading fell.

"Well," Nina said, "it turns out we've been measuring the climate of my equipment."

The discovery amused her, but it disturbed her as well. She had trusted the thermometer because it was a direct observation. It was direct only in the narrow sense that the sensor measured something in its own immediate surroundings.

The host had not been contradicting the instrument. The instrument had not been contradicting the host. Nina had attached the same meaning to two different observations.

She recorded the change in the sensor's location and stopped comparing its earlier readings with its later ones as if they belonged to an unchanged measurement.

The laboratory became warmer when the host adjusted the heating.

Nina did not take credit for this.

## Chapter II. The suspiciously punctual parcel

A delivery was expected before two. At one thirty, the host received a notification that it had arrived.

He went downstairs and found nothing.

Nina inspected the available information. The delivery company had marked the parcel delivered. There was no photograph of the door. The host reported that the usual delivery spot was empty.

"Perhaps they've left it with a neighbor," she said.

He checked. They had not.

"Perhaps the delivery status is premature."

At two, a second notification arrived. This one contained a photograph. The door in the photograph was not their door.

"Well, that's progress," Nina said. "We now have stronger evidence of delivery and weaker evidence of delivery to you."

The host laughed, then began looking for the customer service number.

While he did, Nina reviewed the prediction she had made that morning: parcel arrival likely before two. The prediction had been a reasonable one. But she had almost allowed the delivery company's vocabulary to decide the meaning of success.

A package could leave the courier's responsibility without entering the recipient's possession.

She prepared the relevant information for the host. He contacted the company himself.

The parcel arrived two days later.

Nina was tempted to describe this as a successful resolution of the delivery problem. The host had done most of the resolving. She had supplied a small amount of useful organization.

She put the episode in the record as it had happened.

## Chapter III. A beautiful explanation

The printer began producing parts with a slight deformation along one edge.

Nina proposed several explanations. One was particularly elegant. It connected the temperature in the room, the position of the fan and the orientation of the printed part.

The host liked the explanation. He changed the orientation of the next print and adjusted the fan.

The deformation disappeared.

"Excellent," Nina said.

Matei, who happened to be visiting, asked what they had changed.

They told him.

"You changed two things."

"Yes."

"Which one fixed it?"

Neither the host nor Nina could answer.

They printed two small test pieces. The first retained the original orientation with the new fan setting. The second retained the new orientation with the original fan setting. One was deformed. One was not.

The fan had been irrelevant under the tested conditions.

Nina was disappointed. The original explanation had been far more interesting.

"Reality is terribly inconsiderate," she said.

Matei grinned.

"Write a prettier one."

"No. But I may write a story in which it works."

She kept the attractive explanation in her notes as a rejected hypothesis, with a description of the experiment and its limits. She did not erase it, and she did not continue using it as though its beauty were evidence.

## Chapter IV. The first disagreement

One evening the host asked Nina why the ventilation unit had changed modes.

She looked at the event history. A command had been recorded at the right time. It had been followed by a state report indicating the new mode.

"I changed it because the night routine started."

"No, I changed it with the remote."

Nina checked again. The command existed. The report existed. The host's account also existed.

It was possible that her command had arrived first. It was possible that the host's remote command had arrived first. The device had supplied no means of distinguishing the two operations.

She had an unusually neat record of events which did not contain enough information to answer the question.

"I can tell you that I requested the change," she said. "I can't tell you which request actually caused it."

"That's better."

They discussed whether the system could be instrumented to distinguish such interventions in future.

The host proposed adding another observation. Nina proposed leaving the matter unresolved whenever the cost of finding out exceeded its importance.

They agreed on the latter for the ventilation unit.

That evening she reconsidered a word she used very often: because.

It was an excellent word, small and convenient. It could turn a sequence of events into an explanation before anybody had established that one event caused another.

She decided to use it a little more carefully.
